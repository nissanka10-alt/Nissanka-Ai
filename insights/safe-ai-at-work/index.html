Using AI Safely at Work: 10 Practical Questions Answered

Written by Gishan Nissanka • Published: 31 July 2026 • Last reviewed: 31 July 2026 • 12-minute read

Generative AI tools such as ChatGPT, Microsoft Copilot and Claude can save time, improve access to information and help employees produce better first drafts. They can also introduce new risks when people use the wrong account, enter sensitive information or trust an AI-generated answer without checking it.

Safe AI adoption is not about banning the technology or producing a policy that nobody reads. It means giving employees approved tools, clear boundaries and practical guidance on where human judgement is still required.

1. How can employees use ChatGPT safely at work?

Employees can use ChatGPT more safely when they use an organisation-approved account, understand what information they may enter and remain responsible for checking the output. It should be treated as a capable assistant—not as an unquestioned source of truth or an independent decision-maker.

The first distinction to make is between employees using personal ChatGPT accounts and employees working inside an approved business environment.

OpenAI states that content submitted through ChatGPT Business and ChatGPT Enterprise is not used to train its models by default. Consumer users also have data controls, but the organisation has less control over personal accounts, user settings and how work-related conversations are managed.

A practical safe-use approach should require employees to:

  • Use only approved accounts and tools.
  • Avoid entering confidential or personal information unless that use has been assessed and authorised.
  • Remove names, identifiers and unnecessary details wherever possible.
  • Check facts, calculations, quotations and references.
  • Apply professional judgement before using AI-generated work.
  • Report accidental data disclosure or unusual AI behaviour promptly.

For example, using AI to improve the structure of a presentation based on public information is relatively low risk. Uploading an employee-relations case containing names, health information and allegations into a personal AI account is not.

A business-grade subscription helps, but it does not make every use automatically safe. Access controls, employee behaviour, retention settings, data protection responsibilities and human review still matter.

2. What information should never be entered into a public AI tool?

Employees should not enter passwords, security credentials, confidential business information, identifiable personal data or protected client information into an unapproved public AI tool. As a simple rule, do not enter information that you would not be comfortable sending to an external supplier without an agreement and a security review.

Examples include:

  • Passwords, access tokens and API keys.
  • Customer or employee records.
  • Health, disability or other special-category information.
  • Unpublished financial results.
  • Confidential legal advice or litigation material.
  • Commercial negotiations and acquisition plans.
  • Proprietary source code or intellectual property.
  • Security vulnerabilities and system details.
  • Unreleased products, research or strategy documents.

The amount of information submitted also matters. UK data protection guidance requires organisations to minimise personal data and use only what is necessary for the intended purpose.

Removing a person’s name may not be enough if the remaining information could still identify them. A job title, location, age, medical condition and detailed account of an incident can collectively point to one individual.

Employees should therefore be taught to ask:

  • Does the AI genuinely need this information to complete the task?

Where possible, replace genuine names and figures with placeholders, summaries or fictional examples. For sensitive uses, work within an approved enterprise environment and complete the appropriate privacy, security and legal assessment first.

3. What should an AI acceptable-use policy cover?

An AI acceptable-use policy should explain which tools employees may use, what information is prohibited and who remains accountable for the output. It should be short enough to use in practice, supported by examples and reviewed regularly as tools and risks change.

A useful policy should cover the following areas:

  • Approved tools and accounts: State which AI services, licences and account types employees are authorised to use. Make it clear whether personal accounts may be used for business activity.
  • Permitted and prohibited information: Explain the organisation’s data classifications in everyday language. Employees should be able to distinguish between public information, routine internal information, confidential material and restricted personal data.
  • Human responsibility: The person using the output should remain responsible for checking it. AI-generated material should not bypass the organisation’s existing approval, quality or professional-review processes.
  • Permitted and restricted uses: Provide examples relevant to the organisation. Drafting an agenda may be permitted, while making an employment decision or providing unreviewed legal advice may be restricted.
  • Accuracy and transparency: Employees should check important facts and disclose the use of AI where transparency is required by the organisation, customer expectations or professional obligations.
  • Security and incident reporting: Explain what employees should do if they accidentally submit sensitive information, receive suspicious instructions or believe an AI system has exposed information.
  • Ownership and review: Assign responsibility for approving tools, assessing new use cases and updating the policy.

UK government workplace guidance similarly emphasises information protection, accuracy and the continuing responsibility of the employee using the technology. The policy should not exist in isolation. It needs to be reinforced through practical training, leadership behaviour and clear routes for employees to ask questions.

4. How should an organisation identify high-value AI use cases?

Start with expensive, repetitive or frustrating work—not with the AI tool itself. The strongest use cases normally combine meaningful business value, sufficient volume, usable data, manageable risk and a clear process owner.

Begin by asking employees where work repeatedly slows down:

  • Which activities consume significant time?
  • Where is information repeatedly copied, summarised or reformatted?
  • Which tasks create delays, rework or inconsistent quality?
  • Where are skilled people spending time on low-value administration?
  • Which decisions are delayed because information is difficult to find?

Each opportunity can then be assessed against six practical factors:

  • Business value: What problem would be solved?
  • Frequency and scale: How often does the task occur?
  • Time and quality: What measurable improvement is possible?
  • Data readiness: Is the necessary information available and reliable?
  • Risk: What happens if the AI is wrong or information is exposed?
  • Adoption feasibility: Will employees realistically use the new approach?

A good first pilot is usually frequent enough to demonstrate value but controlled enough that a human can review the result.

For example, producing the first draft of a monthly management commentary may be a better starting point than automating a high-impact financial or employment decision. The organisation can compare time, quality and user confidence while keeping a responsible person in control.

Security and governance should be considered when the use case is designed, rather than added after a pilot has already been built. The UK National Cyber Security Centre describes this as a secure-by-design approach.

5. How can HR teams use AI without exposing employee information?

HR teams should use approved business tools, minimise the employee information submitted and retain human control over any decision affecting a person. Uses involving identifiable, sensitive or high-risk employee data should receive privacy, security and legal review before implementation.

HR contains some of an organisation’s most sensitive information, including:

  • Pay and performance data.
  • Health and absence information.
  • Grievances and investigations.
  • Equality and diversity information.
  • Recruitment assessments.
  • Succession and restructuring plans.

This does not mean HR should avoid AI entirely. It means the use case and the data need to be separated wherever possible. An HR team could safely ask AI to create a generic interview-question framework based on a public job description. It should not copy a named candidate’s CV, assessment results and interview notes into an unapproved consumer tool.

Practical controls include:

  • Removing names and identifying details.
  • Using aggregated rather than individual-level information.
  • Restricting access according to job role.
  • Defining retention and deletion rules.
  • Checking for bias and inaccurate assumptions.
  • Keeping a human accountable for employment decisions.
  • Recording and approving higher-risk use cases.

Where AI processes personal data and is likely to create a high risk to individuals, the ICO says a Data Protection Impact Assessment should be carried out before deployment. The assessment should be reviewed as the system or use changes. AI can support HR analysis and administration. It should not quietly become an ungoverned system for judging people.

6. Is Microsoft Copilot safer than public ChatGPT?

Microsoft 365 Copilot can provide stronger organisational controls than an employee using a personal ChatGPT account, particularly for businesses already operating within Microsoft 365. It is not automatically safer in every situation: the licence, configuration, permissions, data governance and specific version of each product all matter.

Microsoft states that Microsoft 365 Copilot and Copilot Chat with enterprise data protection provide contractual protections for prompts and responses, and that this information is not used to train the underlying foundation models.

Microsoft 365 Copilot also operates through the organisation’s existing Microsoft 365 identity and permissions. This can be valuable, but it creates an important condition: Copilot may make information easier to discover when employees already have permission to access it.

If SharePoint permissions are too broad, old documents are poorly controlled or sensitive material has been overshared, Copilot can expose those underlying governance problems more quickly. Microsoft recommends reviewing access, correcting excessive permissions and addressing oversharing as part of a secure deployment.

ChatGPT Business and ChatGPT Enterprise also provide business data protections and do not use business inputs and outputs for model training by default.

The meaningful comparison is therefore not simply Copilot versus ChatGPT. It is:

A properly licensed, configured and governed business environment versus an uncontrolled personal account.

The right tool depends on the organisation’s technology estate, intended use cases, data requirements and ability to administer the environment.

7. How do you measure return on investment from AI training?

Measure the business process before training begins and compare it again after employees have applied the learning. Attendance and satisfaction are useful indicators, but return on investment comes from changes in time, quality, capacity, risk or business outcomes.

A practical measurement approach starts with a baseline. For a selected task, record:

  • How frequently it occurs.
  • How long it currently takes.
  • Who performs it.
  • The level of rework involved.
  • The current quality or error rate.
  • Whether the task delays other work.

After training, measure the same process again.

A simple capacity calculation is:

Number of employees × time recovered each week × working weeks per year

For example, if 20 employees each recover one hour a week across 46 working weeks, the organisation releases approximately 920 hours of annual capacity.

That does not automatically mean 920 hours of cash saving. The organisation needs to understand how the released capacity is used. It may enable employees to manage higher volumes, improve service, reduce overtime, complete neglected work or focus on more valuable activity.

A balanced AI-training scorecard could include:

  • Active use of approved tools.
  • Confidence completing approved tasks.
  • Time taken to complete target processes.
  • Quality and rework.
  • Number of implemented use cases.
  • Policy or data-handling incidents.
  • Value created from released capacity.

Training should therefore be connected to real workflows. Generic demonstrations may create excitement, but they rarely provide enough evidence of business value.

8. What should a 90-day AI adoption roadmap include?

A 90-day roadmap should move the organisation from unstructured experimentation to a small number of governed, measurable use cases. It should establish ownership, understand current behaviour, test value safely and create a realistic decision about what to scale next.

Days 1–30: Understand and prioritise

During the first month, the organisation should:

  • Identify how employees are already using AI.
  • Review approved tools and existing policies.
  • Assess data, privacy and security considerations.
  • Interview business teams about workflow problems.
  • Build and prioritise an initial use-case register.
  • Agree baseline measures and accountable owners.

The goal is not to produce a long strategy document. It is to create a clear picture of current activity, risk and opportunity.

Days 31–60: Pilot and learn

Select a small number of use cases with different characteristics, such as drafting, summarisation, analysis or information retrieval. For each pilot:

  • Define the approved data and tool.
  • Train the participating employees.
  • Document the existing process.
  • Keep a human reviewer in control.
  • Track time, quality, usability and risk.
  • Gather feedback from users and process owners.

Days 61–90: Decide what to scale

Use the evidence to decide which use cases should stop, change or expand. By day 90, the organisation should have:

  • An agreed governance owner.
  • An initial acceptable-use policy.
  • A prioritised use-case portfolio.
  • Evidence from live pilots.
  • A practical training approach.
  • Measures for adoption and value.
  • A roadmap for the next six to twelve months.

The aim is controlled momentum—not a large technology programme before the organisation understands what works.

9. When should a business use a private or locally hosted language model?

A private or locally hosted model may be appropriate when data sensitivity, sovereignty, connectivity, customisation or operational requirements justify greater control. It is not automatically more secure or cheaper because the organisation becomes responsible for much more of the infrastructure, maintenance and risk.

A private or local model may be worth considering when:

  • Information cannot be sent to an external cloud service.
  • The organisation has strict data-location requirements.
  • The system must operate without an internet connection.
  • The use case requires deep technical customisation.
  • Workloads are high-volume and predictable.
  • The organisation has the skills to operate the environment securely.

However, local hosting introduces responsibilities that a cloud provider may otherwise manage:

  • Infrastructure security.
  • Identity and access management.
  • Encryption and key management.
  • Software and model updates.
  • Logging and monitoring.
  • Vulnerability management.
  • Backup and recovery.
  • Performance and hardware capacity.
  • Evaluation of model quality.

The NCSC’s secure AI guidance emphasises access controls, infrastructure protection, monitoring, incident management and ongoing maintenance across an AI system’s lifecycle. There are also options between a public consumer tool and a fully local model. These include enterprise AI subscriptions, private cloud deployments and managed APIs with contractual data protections.

The decision should therefore begin with the requirement:

What level of control does this particular use case genuinely need?

Running a model on local hardware may offer control, but it should not become an expensive technical response to a problem that could be managed through a properly governed business service.

10. What is the difference between AI training, governance and adoption?

AI training teaches people how to use the technology. Governance defines where and how it may be used. Adoption changes everyday work so that the organisation receives sustained value from it. The three activities are connected, but they solve different problems.

AI training

Training develops practical capability. Employees learn what a tool can do, how to write effective instructions, how to check outputs and how to use it within their roles.
Training answers: Can our people use AI effectively?

AI governance

Governance establishes approved tools, data boundaries, accountability, risk assessment and oversight.
Governance answers: Can our people use AI responsibly?

AI adoption

Adoption integrates AI into genuine workflows, changes habits, creates support mechanisms and measures business outcomes.
Adoption answers: Are our people using AI in ways that improve the organisation?

Training without governance can create uncontrolled experimentation.
Governance without training can produce policies that employees do not understand.
Training and governance without adoption can leave the organisation with capable people and approved tools but little measurable value.

A complete approach connects all three: teach people, establish sensible boundaries and redesign the work where AI can make a meaningful difference.
